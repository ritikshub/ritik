One hour at the tapri

Aug 2026 · 3 min

There is a chai tapri two streets from my flat. It has no name, no board, and no menu. It has a bench with one short leg, a kettle that has outlived three governments, and a man everyone calls Bhaiya. I learned his actual name last month and have already forgotten it, which feels wrong to admit and completely normal at the same time.

I go in the evenings, because my work happens at home now. Remote work sounds like freedom until you notice that the office never closes; it just moves into the room where you sleep. So around six I shut the laptop mid-thought, walk the two streets, and let the tapri hold the thought for me. The first week I carried my phone to the bench and scrolled while I drank. Bhaiya said nothing. He poured, I paid. A clean transaction, no reviews.

It took me longer to notice the system running under the surface. Regulars do not order. He looks up, you nod, and your tea appears — adhaa for the rickshaw-wallah, kadak with extra adrak for the tailor who is always cold, mine in the glass with the chipped rim because I once said the steel tumbler burns my fingers. He holds forty of these standing orders in his head, through power cuts and festival rushes. No app, no reminders, no sync. I build software for a living, and my software forgets things while attached to a database.

The talk there is aggressively unimportant. Rain. Cricket. Tomato prices. One uncle’s court case that has been at “next month” for four years. In a full hour nobody says the word growth, nobody networks, and nobody asks what I do. This took getting used to. At the tapri I am not a software engineer; I am the one who drinks slowly and holds three glasses whenever a scooter needs pushing.

Last Tuesday the power went mid-boil. Nobody reacted. Bhaiya shifted the kettle to the coal angithi he keeps for exactly this, the tailor kept describing his nephew’s wedding, and the tea arrived four minutes late — the only outage in the shop’s recorded history. I have written three incident reports this year for less.

The chai costs ten rupees. The hour costs nothing and returns more than most meetings I attend. This site is where I keep notes on what breaks; the tapri is where I go, one hour a day, to sit inside something that doesn’t. I still cannot remember Bhaiya’s name. I have decided this is fine. He has never once asked for mine.
